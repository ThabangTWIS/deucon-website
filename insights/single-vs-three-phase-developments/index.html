Single-phase or three-phase? A guide for estate and planned unit developers

DevelopmentsBy Deucon16 November 20267 min read

The choice between single-phase and three-phase supplies is made once, early, and then lives in the ground for decades. Get it right and the development handles solar, heat pumps and electric vehicles with ease. Get it wrong and buyers feel it every month.

The basics, briefly

A single-phase supply delivers power on one live conductor at 230 V. A three-phase supply delivers it on three, at 400 V between phases. For the same current per phase, a three-phase supply carries roughly three times the power, and it can run three-phase equipment such as larger pumps, motors and fast electric vehicle chargers.

For a development, the decision happens at two levels:

  • The bulk supply to the development. This is always three-phase, usually at medium voltage, feeding one or more mini-substations.
  • The supply to each unit or erf. This is where the real choice lies: single-phase, three-phase, or a mix depending on unit type.

Designing for demand, not just connections

Residential networks in South Africa are typically sized using NRS 034, which works with the after diversity maximum demand (ADMD): the average peak load per home when many homes share a network, since they do not all peak at the same moment.

The catch is that ADMD values reflect how people used electricity in the past. Today's buyers increasingly add:

  • Rooftop solar and hybrid inverters with batteries
  • Heat pumps, pool pumps and air conditioning in every room
  • Electric vehicle chargers
  • Home offices and workshops

An upmarket estate designed on an outdated ADMD can end up with transformers and cables that are undersized within a few years. Retrofitting a network after roads and paving are finished is slow, disruptive and expensive.

When single-phase per unit makes sense

  • Compact units such as apartments, simplexes and affordable or student housing
  • Developments with modest expected loads and gas for cooking and hot water
  • Projects where keeping connection costs and monthly fixed charges low matters most to buyers

Single-phase units must be spread evenly across the three phases of the network. Poor balancing causes uneven voltages, neutral overloading and nuisance problems that are hard to trace later.

When three-phase per unit pays off

  • Larger freehold homes in lifestyle and security estates, where buyers expect to add solar, batteries and EV charging
  • Solar-ready marketing. Municipal limits on how much generation can connect are generally lower for single-phase supplies, so three-phase lets owners install bigger systems.
  • Electric vehicles. Single-phase home chargers are typically limited to around 7 kW. Faster AC charging needs three-phase.
  • Common facilities such as clubhouses, gatehouses, sewer pump stations, borehole pumps and estate lighting, which usually need three-phase regardless

A practical approach for developers

  1. Involve an electrical engineer at the planning stage, before the site layout is fixed. Mini-substation positions, servitudes and cable routes are much easier to agree early.
  2. Agree the realistic demand per unit type with the engineer, rather than relying only on standard ADMD tables.
  3. Decide the metering model. Individual municipal meters per unit, or a bulk supply with sub-metering by the body corporate or home owners association. Each has different costs, approvals and long-term responsibilities.
  4. Mix where it makes sense. Many developments use single-phase for smaller units and three-phase for larger homes and common facilities.
  5. Future-proof the reticulation. Size cables and kiosks with spare capacity, and leave space for an extra mini-substation if later phases may need it.
  6. Apply to the municipality or Eskom early. Bulk supply applications and network upgrades can take months, and should not hold up occupation.

The bottom line: the cheapest network to build is rarely the cheapest to own. A few extra months of planning and a modest increase in capacity can protect sales, avoid costly retrofits and make the development genuinely ready for solar and electric vehicles.
